Most people, including most psychologists, operate on the assumption that group psychology is to be understood in terms of individual psychology; that individuals have various self-directed drives and that, in group-contexts, these drives are somehow diverted away from their normal paths and pressed into aims that, not being self-directed, are alien to their own. 
This position is understandable, and is at least descriptively correct, where very basic drives are concerned—divines relating to immediate threats to one’s survival and to the prospect of immediate carnal gratification. 
But this methodological stance cannot be reconciled with the incredibly high degree of conformism exhibited by people, even after intellectual shortcomings on their part are taken into account, to other people’s opinions; nor can it be reconciled, even after people’s fear of others is taken into account, with the deference given by people, at the expense of their own gratification, to convention and to pre-existing custom, as well as fleeting but powerful fads. 
Also, given how unintelligent, ignorant, and lacking in moral fiber most individuals are, and yet how much intelligence, knowledge, and moral rigor is embodied in the behavior of collectives, such as corporations and academic disciplines, it is hard to believe that such collectives are the result of various bona fide individuals pursuing their respective paths. It seems rather as though the collective is the primary thing, biologically speaking, and there is an illusion of individual choice and, indeed, of individualism per se. 
A cell is an individual organism. But how well can you understand a cell if you try to understand on its own terms? Pretty well but not maximally well. If you tried to understand its behavior in terms of a drive on its part to live or flourish or reproduce, you would get far; but there would still be some unanswered, and unanswerable, questions. 
But suppose you tried to understand individual cells in the following way. They individualy try to survive, flourish and reproduce to the extent---but only to the extent--that their trying to do so is necessary for the collectives formed thereby to survive and flourish and reproduce. In that case, I would suggest, you will get at least as far as you did with your other hypothesis, and probably further. Cell-individualism can be explained in terms of cell collectivism, but not so much vice versa. 
Similarly, person-individualism can be explained in terms of person-collectivism, but not so much vice versa. Bona fide individuals—people who think for themselves: geniuses, in other words---can be seen as limiting cases of collectives; as collectives of one, perhaps, or as rogue actors; or, most likely, as members of collectives who have an unusual office within that collective and are therefore seen, wrongly, as not being a part of those collectives. And the intense drives that individuals have to survive and experience gratification can be seen as being necessary for the existence for, and therefore explainable in terms of, the collectives to which they belong. Thus, the facts relating to individual psychology can be reconciled with the view that group psychology is primary. 
But the opposite seems not to be true. It is very hard to explain group behavior—be the group in question a mere mob or an organized anti-mob, such as an advanced culture---on the assumption that these collectives represent averages of the behaviors of so many armies of one—of so many self-contained existentialist autonomous deciders. Outside of contexts where there are well-defined and immediate threats to physical well being or where there equally well defined prospects of crude forms of gratification---outside of such context, people are predisposed to give far too much weight, and in far too well-defined ways, to existing beliefs and mores than can be accounted for in terms of the hypothesis that collectives are indeed collectives of bona fide individuals.

Group psychology is more fundamental than individual psychology. Most people, psychologists included, suppose that group psychology ought to be explained through individual psychology. On this view, individuals have a range of self-regarding drives, which, within groups, are redirected from their ordinary courses and made to serve ends foreign to the individuals, since those ends are not self-directed.

The methodological individualist position is understandable. It is at least descriptively right concerning highly basic drives, including responses to immediate dangers to one’s continued survival and responses to an immediate opportunity for bodily pleasure. Yet it cannot account for the extraordinary degree to which people conform to the opinions of others. Even once people’s intellectual limitations are considered, this conformity remains insufficiently explained. Nor can methodological individualism account for people’s submission to convention, inherited practice, and short-lived yet forceful fashions. People yield to each of these even at the cost of their own satisfaction, and this submission remains inadequately explained even after fear of other people is considered.

Methodological individualism also has difficulty explaining the intelligence, knowledge, and moral strictness expressed in collective conduct. Most people are not intelligent, are ignorant, and lack moral firmness, yet collectives can display considerable intelligence, knowledge, and moral strictness in their conduct. Courts and scientific communities are examples of such collectives. It is difficult to believe that they arise merely from distinct bona fide individuals each following a separate course. The collective therefore seems biologically prior, while individual choice and individualism itself each appear, in part, to be illusions.

Cells demonstrate the explanatory priority of collectivism over individualism. A cell is an individual organism and can be understood fairly well by considering it on its own terms, but it cannot be understood as fully as possible merely in this way. Much of its behavior can be explained by its drives to remain alive, prosper, and reproduce. However, explaining it exclusively through its own drives to live, prosper, and reproduce leaves certain questions unresolved, and the questions left unresolved by the individual-cell hypothesis cannot be answered from within that hypothesis.

Cells may instead be understood as seeking survival, flourishing, and reproduction only insofar as this is required for the survival, flourishing, and reproduction of the collectives they constitute. Individual cells seek survival, flourishing, and reproduction only to the degree required for their collectives to survive, flourish, and reproduce, and do not seek these beyond what is so required. The collectivist hypothesis about cells would explain at least as much as the individual-cell hypothesis and would likely explain more. Cell individualism can be explained through cell collectivism, whereas cell collectivism cannot be explained nearly as well through cell individualism.

Likewise, person-individualism can be explained through person-collectivism, while person-collectivism is not nearly as explainable in terms of person-individualism. Bona fide individuals may be understood as limiting cases of collectives. Such individuals are persons who think independently, and geniuses are bona fide individuals in this sense. A bona fide individual may be regarded as a collective containing one member or as a dissident actor. More probably, however, such an individual is a member of a collective who occupies an unusual role within it and is mistakenly regarded as outside the collective to which that person belongs.

Individuals’ powerful drives for survival and gratification can be understood as necessary to the existence of the collectives of which they are members. These drives are therefore explainable in terms of the collectives to which they belong. The facts of individual psychology can thus be reconciled with the claim that group psychology is primary.

The reverse explanatory program fails: collective behavior cannot generally be explained by assuming that collectives are composed of bona fide individuals. Explaining group behavior is exceedingly difficult if a collective is assumed to be an average of the conduct of many armies of one. An army of one is a self-contained, existentialist, autonomous chooser, and the relevant assumption treats a collective as made up of many such choosers. This explanatory problem arises whether the group is simply a crowd or an organized anti-crowd, such as a constitutional state.

Except where immediate physical danger is clearly defined, people are disposed to give existing beliefs and mores too much weight for individualist explanations to accommodate. The same is true except where prospects of crude gratification are equally clear and immediate. Outside these circumstances, people assign far too much importance to existing beliefs and mores, and do so in ways too determinate to be explained by the hypothesis that collectives consist of bona fide individuals.
